INTERESTING & SOCIAL
Ulugbek Davlatov
WHY ARE WE BECOMING MORE LONELY?
How constant connection can exist alongside a growing sense of isolation.
LONELINESS • FRIENDSHIP • SOCIAL MEDIA • CONNECTION
A person can wake up to twenty messages, spend the day
surrounded by colleagues, scroll through hundreds of
photographs, and still go to bed feeling strangely alone. This
is one of the contradictions of modern life. We have more
ways to contact one another than any generation before us,
yet many people say that deep connection feels harder to
find. Loneliness is not simply the absence of people. It is the
feeling that the connection we need is missing. You can feel
lonely in an empty apartment, but you can also feel lonely at
a crowded dinner table. The question, then, is not only how
many people are around us. It is whether we feel seen,
understood and emotionally close to them.
CONNECTED ALL DAY, CLOSE TO NO ONE
Modern technology has made communication extraordinarily
easy. A friend living thousands of kilometres away can reply
within seconds. Families can share photographs every day.
Group chats allow dozens of people to remain in constant
contact. These are genuine benefits. Yet frequent contact is
not automatically deep contact. A stream of short messages
may tell us what someone ate, bought or watched without
revealing what they fear, regret or hope for. We can know
many details about another person's day and still know very
little about their inner life. Digital communication often
works best when it supports real relationships. The problem
begins when it becomes a substitute for the slower forms of
attention from which intimacy grows.
THE EMPTY SEAT IN A CROWDED CAFE
Imagine someone sitting alone in a busy cafe. Around them,
friends are laughing, couples are talking and a waiter is
moving between tables. The person is not physically isolated,
yet the room may make their loneliness feel sharper. Human
beings naturally compare their situation with what they see
around them. A quiet evening can feel peaceful until a screen
shows a party we were not invited to. A single weekend at
home can suddenly look like evidence that everyone else has
a more exciting social life. What we see, however, is usually
a selected moment rather than a complete life.
FRIENDSHIP NEEDS UNPLANNED TIME
Many strong friendships are built through repetition rather
than dramatic moments. Children become close because they
see one another every day. University students talk after
class, share meals and waste time together. Young colleagues
may become friends simply because they take the same bus
home. Adult life can remove these natural opportunities.
People change jobs, move to different neighbourhoods,
marry, raise children and become busy. Friendship then
requires planning. A conversation that once happened
automatically now needs a free evening, matching schedules
and perhaps a journey across the city. When every meeting
must be organised, relationships can slowly become less
spontaneous.
BUSYNESS CAN HIDE LONELINESS
A full calendar can make someone look socially successful
while hiding emotional isolation. Work meetings, classes,
errands and family responsibilities put people in contact with
others, but these interactions may remain functional. We
discuss deadlines, traffic, prices and tasks. We may go
through an entire day without saying anything personally
meaningful. Busyness can even become a way of avoiding
loneliness. If every hour is occupied, there is less time to
notice what is missing. The feeling often appears when the
noise stops: late at night, on a quiet Sunday, or after returning
home from an event where we spoke to many people but
connected with no one.
WHY SMALL TALK STILL MATTERS
Deep conversation is valuable, but not every social
interaction needs to reveal our deepest feelings. Researchers
and everyday experience both suggest that small connections
matter too. Greeting a neighbour, joking with a shop assistant
or exchanging a few words with someone at the gym can
create a sense of belonging. These weak social ties remind us
that we are part of a wider human environment. Modern
convenience sometimes removes them. We order food
without speaking to anyone, use self-service machines and
work from home. Each change may save time, but together
they can reduce the tiny interactions that once gave ordinary
days a social texture.
THE FRIEND WHO STOPPED CALLING
Loneliness is sometimes created not by having no
relationships, but by watching an important one slowly
disappear. Two friends may once have spoken every evening.
Then work becomes demanding. One moves away. Messages
become shorter. A week passes, then a month. Neither person
is angry. Nothing dramatic happens. The friendship simply
receives less attention until both people begin to feel
awkward about the silence. This kind of loss can be difficult
because there is no clear ending. The person still exists, and
the friendship may still matter, but the old closeness has
become a memory.
SOCIAL MEDIA'S STRANGE MIRROR
Social media can connect people, introduce communities and
help old friends find one another. At the same time, it can
become a strange mirror in which we compare our ordinary
lives with other people's edited highlights. We see weddings
but not arguments, holidays but not debt, smiling friends but
not the quiet hours after everyone goes home. When people
forget this selection process, they may interpret other
people's visible happiness as proof of their own social failure.
The result is not always envy. Sometimes it is the quieter
thought: Why does everyone seem to belong somewhere
except me?
MOVING AWAY CHANGES MORE THAN AN
ADDRESS
Migration, study and career opportunities allow people to
build new lives, but moving can quietly break a social
network that took years to create. In a hometown, a person
may know the barber, the neighbour, the former teacher and
the friend who lives three streets away. In a new city, even
simple companionship must be rebuilt. Adults often
underestimate how long this takes. Friendship rarely appears
after one conversation. It grows through repeated encounters,
shared experiences and trust. A new place may be exciting
and lonely at the same time.
THE COURAGE TO SAY I MISS YOU
One reason loneliness survives is that admitting it can feel
embarrassing. People may worry that saying 'I feel lonely'
sounds like saying 'Nobody likes me.' So they remain silent.
They wait for other people to call first. They assume a busy
friend is uninterested. Yet many relationships are revived by
surprisingly simple sentences: 'I haven't seen you in a long
time.' 'Do you want to have coffee?' 'I miss talking to you.'
Vulnerability involves risk, because the invitation may not be
returned. But connection also requires someone to make the
first move.
QUALITY IS DIFFERENT FROM QUANTITY
A large social circle does not guarantee emotional closeness,
and a small circle does not guarantee loneliness. Some
people are happiest with two or three trusted relationships.
Others need a wider community. The important question is
whether the relationships match the person's emotional
needs. One honest conversation can sometimes provide more
connection than an evening spent with twenty acquaintances.
This is why solving loneliness is not simply a matter of
telling people to socialise more. The kind of interaction
matters.
SOLITUDE IS NOT THE ENEMY
Being alone and being lonely are different experiences.
Solitude can be restorative. Reading alone, walking without
company or drinking coffee in silence can give the mind
space to recover. A person who chooses solitude may feel
deeply connected to others and simply enjoy private time.
Loneliness, by contrast, contains a sense of unwanted
distance. Understanding this difference matters because a
healthy life needs both connection and the ability to be alone
without feeling abandoned.
BUILDING A SOCIAL LIFE SLOWLY
There is rarely a dramatic cure for loneliness. More often,
connection is rebuilt through repeated ordinary actions. Join
the same class each week. Walk with a colleague after work.
Call a relative instead of sending another reaction emoji.
Invite someone for tea even if the meeting will last only
thirty minutes. Become a familiar face somewhere. Real
friendship usually needs time, and time needs repetition. The
first conversation may be forgettable. The tenth may become
important.
THE MESSAGE YOU ALMOST DID NOT SEND
Imagine opening an old chat and seeing the name of someone
you once knew well. You type, 'How have you been?' Then
you delete it. It has been too long, you think. It might be
strange. Perhaps tomorrow. But perhaps the other person is
looking at the same silent conversation and thinking exactly
the same thing. Modern loneliness can be complicated, but
sometimes the distance between two people is smaller than it
appears. Sometimes it is only one message, one invitation or
one honest sentence. We cannot keep every friendship, and
no person can remove all loneliness from another person's
life. But we can resist the idea that connection should happen
automatically. Relationships are living things. They need
attention, time and courage. In a world where contacting
someone has never been easier, perhaps the harder skill is
learning how to truly reach them.
