INTERESTING & SOCIAL
Ulugbek Davlatov
WHY DO WE MISS THE PAST SO MUCH?
The strange power of nostalgia and why yesterday can sometimes feel warmer than today.
NOSTALGIA • MEMORY • CHILDHOOD • EMOTION
There is a strange thing that happens when September
arrives. The mornings become cooler. The light changes. The
air begins to smell different. Somewhere, children are
walking to school with new notebooks in their bags. A
familiar song plays unexpectedly in a café, and suddenly you
are no longer standing in the present. For a few seconds, you
are sixteen again. You remember the classroom, the old
wooden desk, the friend who always sat beside you, and the
teacher whose voice you thought you had forgotten. You
may even remember small things that seemed completely
unimportant at the time: the sound of rain against the school
windows, the smell of wet coats in the corridor, or the
excitement of seeing someone you secretly liked. Nothing
extraordinary happened. Yet the memory feels precious. This
feeling has a name: nostalgia.
THE PAST WAS NOT PERFECT
When people become nostalgic, they often say things such
as, “Life was better back then.” But was it? Think carefully
about your school years. There were probably boring lessons,
difficult exams, arguments with friends, embarrassing
moments and mornings when you desperately wanted to stay
in bed. Yet years later, the unpleasant details often become
weaker while the beautiful ones remain surprisingly clear.
Our memory is not a video camera. It does not store every
experience exactly as it happened. Instead, the brain
constantly selects, changes and reconstructs memories. This
means that when we remember the past, we are not
necessarily visiting the past exactly as it was. We are visiting
a version of it. And that version can become warmer with
time.
A SONG CAN OPEN A DOOR
Music is one of the most powerful triggers of nostalgia.
Imagine that you are shopping when a song begins playing.
You have not heard it for ten years. Immediately, something
changes. Perhaps you remember sitting in the back seat of
your father's car. Maybe you remember a university
dormitory, a summer holiday, your first job, or someone you
once loved. For a moment, the memory is almost physical.
This happens partly because music is strongly connected
with emotion and autobiographical memory. Songs often
become attached to particular periods of our lives, especially
adolescence and early adulthood, when many important
experiences happen for the first time: first serious friendship,
first independence, first heartbreak, first major success, first
time leaving home. Years later, the opening seconds of a
song can unlock an entire emotional world.
WHY SEPTEMBER FEELS DIFFERENT
Nostalgia is often connected to seasons. September, in
particular, carries powerful associations for many people
because it represents transition. Summer is ending. Schools
reopen. Universities begin new semesters. People return from
holidays. The days slowly become shorter. Even adults who
have not entered a classroom for decades can experience a
strange back-to-school feeling when autumn begins. A cool
morning can remind someone of walking to school at the age
of twelve. The smell of new books can bring back memories
of September shopping with a parent. Rain on a window may
remind someone of sitting in a classroom and waiting for the
final bell. Our surroundings act like invisible keys. A smell,
temperature, sound or colour can open a memory that has
been locked away for years.
THE HOUSE THAT BECAME SMALLER
Many adults experience something surprising when they
return to their childhood home after being away for years.
Everything seems smaller. The bedroom that once felt
enormous suddenly looks tiny. The street that seemed
endless when you were eight can be crossed in two minutes.
The playground that once represented your entire world may
now look ordinary. The place has changed, of course. But so
have you. As children, we experience places differently
because our world is physically and psychologically smaller.
A neighbourhood may contain almost everything we know:
family, friends, school, shops, games and adventures. When
adults return, they are not simply looking at buildings. They
are comparing two versions of themselves: the child who
lived there and the adult who has returned. Perhaps this
explains why visiting an old place can be surprisingly
emotional. We realise that the place still exists, but the life
we had there does not.
WE MISS PEOPLE, BUT ALSO OURSELVES
Sometimes when we say, “I miss those days,” what we really
mean is, “I miss who I was in those days.” This is an
important difference. Perhaps you miss university because
life seemed full of possibilities. You had little money, your
room was terrible and exams were stressful—but the future
felt enormous. Perhaps you miss childhood summers because
you were able to enjoy an afternoon without thinking about
bills, careers or responsibilities. Perhaps you miss an old
friend not because you want the friendship back exactly as it
was, but because that person reminds you of a younger
version of yourself. People from our past become witnesses
to versions of us that no longer exist. A childhood friend
remembers you before your career. A former classmate
remembers your terrible haircut. An old neighbour
remembers you riding a bicycle outside until sunset. These
people carry pieces of our personal history. That can make
losing contact with them feel like losing part of ourselves.
THE FIRST TIME IS POWERFUL
Childhood and adolescence contain an unusual number of
first experiences: the first day of school, the first best friend,
the first trip without your parents, the first time someone
breaks your heart, the first time you earn your own money,
the first time you realise adults do not know everything. First
experiences require more attention because they are
unfamiliar. The brain has fewer existing patterns with which
to compare them. Adult life, however, often contains
repetition. You may travel to work using the same road
hundreds of times. You may buy groceries from the same
supermarket, sit at the same desk and drink coffee from the
same cup. Because these experiences are familiar, fewer of
them become distinctive memories. This may partly explain
why childhood can feel so long in retrospect while adulthood
appears to accelerate. It is not necessarily because childhood
contained more happiness. It contained more novelty.
WHY DIFFICULT TIMES CAN BECOME BEAUTIFUL
MEMORIES
Nostalgia can perform another interesting trick. It can soften
difficult experiences. Ask someone about their student years,
and they might laugh while describing how five friends
shared a tiny apartment, survived on cheap food and had
almost no money. At the time, it probably was not funny. But
years later, the hardship becomes part of a beloved story. The
uncomfortable apartment becomes “our little place.” The
terrible old car becomes “the car we had all our adventures
in.” The stressful part-time job becomes “where we all met.”
Time changes the emotional colour of experiences. Pain may
remain, but it often becomes mixed with affection because
we know the difficult period eventually ended.
NOSTALGIA IS NOT ONLY SADNESS
People sometimes assume nostalgia is unhealthy because it
involves looking backwards. But nostalgia is not simply
sadness. It is usually bittersweet. We feel happiness because
something beautiful happened, and sadness because it cannot
happen in exactly the same way again. That combination can
be surprisingly comforting. Research on nostalgia has
suggested that nostalgic reflection can strengthen feelings of
social connection and give people a greater sense of
continuity between their past and present selves. In difficult
periods, remembering meaningful experiences can remind us
that our current situation is only one chapter of a much
longer story. We have survived other chapters. We have
loved people. We have belonged somewhere. We have
changed before. We can change again.
THE DANGER OF LIVING BACKWARDS
However, nostalgia has another side. The past can become so
beautiful in memory that the present begins to look
disappointing by comparison. Someone might constantly
think about an old relationship and forget why it ended.
Another person may insist that their school years were the
happiest period of their life and stop creating new
experiences. Someone else might spend hours looking at old
photographs, wishing desperately to return to a world that no
longer exists. This is where nostalgia can become a trap. The
purpose of memory is not to provide us with a permanent
home. We are allowed to visit. But we cannot live there.
OLD PHOTOGRAPHS
Perhaps nothing demonstrates nostalgia better than an old
photograph. You open a drawer and find a picture taken
fifteen years ago. There are six people in it. One is now
living abroad. One has married. One has disappeared from
your life completely. Perhaps one has died. And there you
are in the middle, smiling at the camera without knowing
anything about what will happen next. That may be the most
emotional part. The person in the photograph does not know
the future. They do not know which friendships will survive,
which 
dreams 
will 
disappear, 
which 
unexpected
opportunities will arrive or which ordinary days will later
become precious memories. You know. They do not. For a
moment, you may wish you could enter the photograph and
say something to your younger self: “Enjoy this.” “Don't
worry so much.” “Tell that person how you feel.” Maybe you
would say nothing. Maybe you would simply watch.
TODAY IS ALSO DISAPPEARING
There is an uncomfortable truth hidden inside nostalgia. The
ordinary life we are living today will one day become “the
past.” The room you are sitting in may someday exist only in
photographs. The people you see every week may eventually
live in different cities. Your parents will become older.
Children will grow. Friends will change. The café you
always visit may close. The phone in your hand will look
ridiculously old-fashioned. Even your current worries may
become things you barely remember. We often recognise the
beauty of a period only after it has ended. Perhaps that is one
of nostalgia's most useful lessons. It teaches us to notice
ordinary life before it becomes memory.
ONE ORDINARY SEPTEMBER
Imagine a student walking home on a rainy September
afternoon. Nothing important happens. Classes finish. The
student complains about homework, buys something small
from a shop and walks home with a friend. They talk about
teachers, classmates and plans for the weekend. A car passes
through a puddle. They laugh. At home, someone is cooking
dinner. A schoolbag is dropped beside the door. The evening
continues. There is nothing historic about the day. No one
takes a photograph. No one writes about it in a diary. Twenty
years later, however, rain begins falling on another
September afternoon. For reasons the person cannot fully
explain, that forgotten walk home suddenly returns: the wet
street, the friend's voice, the schoolbag, the warm light
coming from the kitchen. For several seconds, they would
give almost anything to experience that completely ordinary
afternoon one more time. That is nostalgia. It is not always a
desire to return to a better world. Sometimes it is simply the
sudden realisation that something ordinary was beautiful
because it did not last. Perhaps the best response is not to
become sad about yesterday, but to look more carefully at
today. Somewhere inside the ordinary life you are living
right now are moments that your future self will desperately
miss.
